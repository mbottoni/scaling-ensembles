\begin{abstract}
Deep ensembles improve accuracy and calibration by averaging predictions of
independently trained neural networks.  A common concern is that
overparameterized networks converge to functionally similar solutions,
undermining ensemble diversity as capacity grows.  We investigate this
systematically across six benchmarks (MNIST to CIFAR-10) spanning a full
difficulty range, using CNN and MLP models at widths covering 27K to
9.6M parameters.  Our main finding is that \emph{pairwise disagreement,
ensemble accuracy gain, and calibration benefit are governed by task
difficulty, not parameter count}:  MNIST shows only 2.3--3.3\% disagreement
and $<0.6$\,pp gain at all widths; CIFAR-10 sustains $25$--$31\%$
disagreement and $3$--$4$\,pp gain across a $250\times$ parameter range.
Per-class error rate predicts per-class disagreement within CIFAR-10 with
Pearson $r=0.982$ ($p<10^{-30}$), ruling out dataset-size or resolution
confounds.  Bootstrap tests confirm all between-dataset differences at
$p<0.001$; pairwise error covariance on CIFAR-10 is flat at
$\approx9\,\mathrm{pp}^2$ across widths, showing correlated failures do
not grow with capacity.  A second finding is an overconfidence scaling
law: single-model ECE on CIFAR-10 grows from 7\% to 22\% as width
increases, while ensemble ECE stays at 2--8\%.  We also show that two
narrow models outperform one wide model at the same parameter budget
(+1.4--3.3\,pp), and identify a diversity--barrier dissociation:
loss-landscape barriers collapse with width, yet functional diversity
is preserved.  These results imply that deep ensembles remain practically
valuable for large overparameterized models on genuinely hard tasks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deep ensembles~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple} combine predictions of
$M$ independently trained neural networks.  Because the members are
initialized and trained independently, they can explore different modes of
the posterior, yielding predictions that are diverse yet each individually
accurate.  Ensembles consistently outperform single models on both
accuracy and calibration~\citep{ovadia2019can,gustafsson2020evaluating},
and retain their advantage under distribution
shift~\citep{hendrycks2019robustness}.

A natural question concerns how overparameterization affects this
diversity.  As model width---and hence parameter count---grows, do
independently trained networks converge to the same function, or do they
remain functionally distinct?  Fort, Hu, and
Lakshminarayanan~\citeyearpar{fort2019deep} showed that models starting
from different random initializations tend to land in distinct
\emph{function-space} modes rather than the same mode, even when they are
weight-space-connected by a low-loss path.  Neural tangent kernel (NTK)
theory~\citep{jacot2018neural} predicts that infinitely-wide networks
follow a deterministic kernel machine dynamics, suggesting eventual
convergence.  Empirically, Entezari et al.~\citeyearpar{entezari2022role}
found that sufficiently wide networks are linearly mode-connected after a
permutation alignment, consistent with weight-space convergence.

We ask a complementary question: does width affect the \emph{functional}
diversity of independently trained networks, and does the answer depend on
task difficulty?

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Main contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We show that prediction disagreement and ensemble accuracy gain are
        stable across a $250\times$ parameter-count range on CIFAR-10
        (${\approx}27\%$ disagreement, $3$--$4$ pp gain at all widths),
        but nearly absent on easy benchmarks: FashionMNIST shows
        ${\approx}7\%$ disagreement and $<1$ pp gain; MNIST shows only
        ${\approx}3\%$ disagreement and $<0.6$ pp gain despite spanning
        27K to 1.9M parameters.  Image resolution and dataset size do not
        explain the gap---MNIST and FashionMNIST share the same format.

  \item We quantify the calibration benefit of ensembles via ECE: on
        hard tasks (CIFAR-10), single-model ECE \emph{grows} from 7\% to 22\%
        as width increases (models become more overconfident), while two-member
        ensembles stabilize ECE at 2--8\% across all widths---a reduction of
        5--14\,pp.  On easy tasks the reduction is $<2$ pp.

  \item We characterize ensemble-size scaling: on CIFAR-10 the marginal
        benefit of the $M$-th member is similar across all widths,
        confirming that diversity is \emph{maintained} at higher
        parameter counts.

  \item We identify a \textbf{diversity--barrier dissociation}: on hard
        tasks (STL-10, CIFAR-10), linear interpolation barriers collapse
        with width, yet prediction diversity persists.  This shows that
        weight-space connectivity does not imply functional similarity.

  \item We provide within-dataset validation: per-class error rate predicts
        per-class disagreement within CIFAR-10 with Pearson $r = 0.982$
        ($p < 10^{-30}$), confirming that difficulty---not dataset or
        architecture---governs diversity at fine granularity.

  \item We show an ensemble compute efficiency advantage: two smaller
        models outperform one wider model at the same parameter budget on
        hard tasks ($+1.4$--$3.3$ pp), demonstrating a practical scaling
        law for ensemble deployment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Deep ensembles.}
\citet{lakshminarayanan2017simple} proposed training multiple networks
from different initializations and averaging softmax outputs.  Subsequent
work established that they outperform Bayesian approximations such as
dropout, variational inference, and SWAG on accuracy, calibration, and
OOD detection~\citep{ovadia2019can,gustafsson2020evaluating}.
\citet{wilson2020case} argue that ensembles provide a practical and
compelling alternative to single-model Bayesian inference.
\citet{ashukha2020pitfalls} study evaluation protocols for ensemble
methods and show that careful comparison requires accounting for total
compute, not just number of parameters.  Efficient ensemble alternatives
include \emph{BatchEnsemble}~\citep{wen2020batchensemble}, which trains
multiple ``rank-1'' perturbations of shared weights.  Our work is
complementary: we study whether diversity exists for standard deep
ensembles as a function of task difficulty, not whether it can be
more cheaply approximated.

\paragraph{Loss landscape and mode structure.}
\citet{fort2019deep} argued that deep ensembles work well because
independent initializations land in distinct function-space modes,
whereas subspace methods (e.g., SWAG, dropout) remain in one mode.
\citet{garipov2018loss} and \citet{frankle2020linear} showed that pairs of
minima can be connected by low-loss curves or, for overparameterized
networks, even by straight lines, motivating the study of linear mode
connectivity.  \citet{entezari2022role} conjectured that SGD solutions
become linearly mode-connected after permutation alignment as width
increases.  Model merging methods~\citep{wortsman2022model} leverage
this connectivity to combine models without ensembling.  Our
diversity--barrier dissociation shows that low barriers (connectivity)
do not imply functional similarity, explaining why merging does not
always match ensemble performance on hard tasks.

\paragraph{Overparameterization and NTK theory.}
\citet{jacot2018neural} showed that infinitely-wide networks converge to
kernel machines with a fixed NTK, suggesting eventual functional
convergence.  \citet{lee2019wide} extended this to finite-width networks
with appropriate learning-rate scaling.  In practice, however,
neural networks are used in the \emph{feature-learning} regime far from
the NTK limit, where they can find diverse solutions.  Our empirical
results show that even at 9.6M parameters---where barriers have collapsed
to near zero---functional diversity on CIFAR-10 remains intact, consistent
with operating in the feature-learning regime.

\paragraph{Error decomposition and diversity.}
The classical bias-variance-covariance decomposition for
ensembles~\citep{krogh1995neural} formalizes that ensemble gain comes from
the variance term (individual errors) and is reduced by the covariance
term (correlated errors).  Our work operationalizes this decomposition
through pairwise error covariance, ECE reduction, and ensemble-size scaling.
\citet{fort2019deep} showed that models from different random
initializations occupy distinct modes in function space; our contribution
is to show this mode separation is governed by task difficulty and persists
under increasing width.

\paragraph{Dataset difficulty and Bayes error.}
The Bayes error of a task sets an upper bound on functional agreement
among near-optimal classifiers.  Hard tasks with high Bayes error leave
room for many diverse near-optimal solutions; easy tasks force
convergence.  Our Proposition~\ref{prop:multiplicity}
formalizes this for near-optimal classifiers with independent errors.

\paragraph{Uncertainty quantification.}
Beyond accuracy, ensembles are known to improve uncertainty estimates.
\citet{guo2017calibration} showed that modern neural networks are
overconfident; \citet{lakshminarayanan2017simple} demonstrated that
ensembles reduce calibration error.  Our work shows that this calibration
benefit is gated on task difficulty and is complementary to (but distinct
from) temperature scaling~\citep{guo2017calibration}.

\section{Background and Metrics}
\label{sec:background}

Let a network $f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^K$ map inputs to
logits, and define $p_\theta(y \mid x) = \mathrm{softmax}(f_\theta(x))_y$.
A deep ensemble of $M$ members averages predictive distributions:
\[
  p_{\mathrm{ens}}(y \mid x)
  = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M p_{\theta_m}(y \mid x).
\]

\paragraph{Metrics.}
For a pair of independently trained models $a,b$ evaluated on a test set
of $N$ examples we compute:
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{agreement}
  &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_n \mathbf{1}\{\hat{y}_a(x_n) = \hat{y}_b(x_n)\},\\
  \mathrm{JS}(p_a,p_b)
  &= \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(p_a\|\tfrac{p_a+p_b}{2})
   + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(p_b\|\tfrac{p_a+p_b}{2}),\\
  \mathrm{gain}(a,b)
  &= \mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{ens}} - \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{acc}_a + \mathrm{acc}_b).
\end{align*}
We also report the \emph{linear interpolation barrier}: for
$\theta(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)\theta_a + \alpha\theta_b$, $\alpha\in[0,1]$,
the barrier is $\max_\alpha \ell(\theta(\alpha)) - \ell(\theta_a)$.
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is computed with 15 equal-width
confidence bins:
\[
  \mathrm{ECE}
  = \sum_{j=1}^{15} \frac{|B_j|}{N}
    \bigl|\mathrm{acc}(B_j) - \mathrm{conf}(B_j)\bigr|,
\]
where $B_j$ is the set of examples whose predicted confidence falls in
bin $j$.  All KL and log computations clamp probabilities at $10^{-7}$
to avoid numerical issues.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Architecture.}
We use a \textsc{SmallCNN} with two convolutional blocks and a two-layer
classifier head.  The \emph{width} multiplier $w \in \{16,32,64,128,256\}$
controls channel counts and classifier size, yielding 38\,K to 9.6\,M
parameters.  We also experiment with a small patch-transformer classifier
at widths 64--512.

\paragraph{Training.}
All models are trained with AdamW (lr$\,{=}10^{-3}$, weight
decay$\,{=}10^{-4}$) for 40 epochs with gradient clipping at norm~1.
Ten random seeds are used per width for CIFAR-10 (the primary benchmark);
three to five seeds are used for other datasets.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the six benchmarks.  We order them
roughly by difficulty (measured by the best single-model accuracy achieved
in our sweep).  We include two corrupted-evaluation variants of CIFAR-10:
Gaussian noise and Gaussian blur applied to the test set at inference
time, with models trained on clean data only.

The contrast is striking.  MNIST shows the extreme easy-task regime:
disagreement is only 2.3--3.3\% and ensemble gain is below 0.6\,pp at
all widths from 27K to 1.9M parameters.  FashionMNIST (harder than MNIST
but easier than CIFAR-10) shows 6--7\% disagreement and gain below 1\,pp.
On CIFAR-10, disagreement fluctuates around 25--31\% and ensemble gain
remains 3--4\,pp across the full 250$\times$ parameter range.  Neither
metric collapses with width on the harder benchmark.  Notably, MNIST and
FashionMNIST share the same image size and training set size, yet show
substantially different diversity levels, confirming that intrinsic task
difficulty—not dataset size or image resolution—governs the result.

\textbf{Cross-dataset correlation.}  Averaging over all widths and
seeds per dataset, the Pearson correlation between mean per-dataset
error rate and mean pairwise disagreement across five benchmarks is
$r = 0.94$ (Spearman $\rho = 0.90$, $p = 0.037$).  The ranking from
easiest to hardest---MNIST~$\to$ FashionMNIST~$\to$ SVHN~$\to$ CIFAR-10
$\approx$ STL-10---matches the ranking by disagreement exactly.

\textbf{Statistical significance.}  Bootstrap permutation tests (2000
samples) confirm that CIFAR-10 disagreement significantly exceeds
FashionMNIST disagreement at every matching width ($p < 0.001$ at
$w \in \{16, 64, 128\}$), as does ensemble gain ($p < 0.001$ throughout).

\textbf{Per-class validation.}  The task-difficulty hypothesis predicts
that, within CIFAR-10, harder classes should show higher pairwise
disagreement.  We compute per-class error rate and disagreement for all
pairs at all widths ($50 \times 10 = 500$ data points).  The Pearson
correlation between per-class error rate and per-class disagreement is
$r = 0.982$ (Spearman $\rho = 0.978$, $p < 10^{-30}$).  Hard classes such
as \emph{cat} (45\% error, 43\% disagreement) and \emph{dog} (36\%,
36\%) show far higher diversity than easy classes such as
\emph{automobile} (16\%, 18\%) and \emph{ship} (16\%, 18\%).  This
within-dataset validation rules out confounders like image resolution or
dataset size.

\textbf{Error decomposition.}  Computing the pairwise error covariance
$\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{1}_{a\text{ wrong}}, \mathbf{1}_{b\text{ wrong}})$
for all CIFAR-10 pairs reveals that this covariance is $8.8$--$10.0$\,pp$^2$
and \emph{flat across widths}.  This means that the amount of
\emph{correlated error} between pairs of CIFAR-10 models does not grow with
capacity—a direct indicator that diversity is not squeezed out by
overparameterization.

Figure~\ref{fig:diversity_ci} shows these trends with shaded $\pm$1 std
bands, alongside STL-10 for comparison.  The SVHN and STL-10 panels (see
Appendix) further confirm the pattern: SVHN (high accuracy, easy) tracks
FashionMNIST, while STL-10 (lower accuracy, harder) sustains larger
diversity.

\subsection{Calibration: Ensembles vs.\ Temperature Scaling}
\label{sec:calibration}

Figure~\ref{fig:ece} compares three calibration strategies at each width on
FashionMNIST and CIFAR-10: uncalibrated individual models (blue),
temperature-scaled single models (green), and two-member ensembles (orange).
Temperature scaling finds an optimal scalar temperature $T$ on a 50\%
calibration split and is applied to the remaining 50\%; this gives a fair
estimate of its practical performance.

\textit{CIFAR-10:} A striking capacity-dependent pattern emerges: single-model
ECE grows monotonically from 7.2\% at width~16 to 22.3\% at width~256.
Overparameterized models become more overconfident even as they achieve similar
accuracy, consistent with feature-learning dynamics that amplify prediction
confidence.  Temperature scaling neutralizes this overconfidence to $\approx 2\%$
ECE at all widths.  Two-member ensembles achieve $2$--$8\%$ ECE---slightly worse
than temperature scaling for calibration alone, but without requiring held-out
calibration data and while simultaneously providing 3--4\,pp accuracy gains.

\textit{FashionMNIST:} Individual models are well-calibrated even without
post-hoc correction (ECE 1--5\%); both methods reduce ECE to $<3\%$, with
negligible differences between them.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../outputs/series/paper-plots/overconfidence_vs_width.png}
\caption{Left: single-model ECE grows monotonically with model width on
CIFAR-10 (from 7.2\% at 38K to 22.3\% at 9.6M parameters) but stays
low on FashionMNIST.  Right: while temperature scaling fully neutralizes
overconfidence ($\approx 2\%$ ECE at all widths), two-member ensembles
partially reduce ECE (2--8\%) without requiring held-out calibration data.}
\label{fig:ece}
\end{figure}

This result reveals a novel overconfidence scaling law: on hard tasks,
individual model ECE grows with capacity despite stable accuracy,
indicating that wider models commit more strongly to specific error
patterns.  The overconfidence is further confirmed by NLL: single-model
NLL on CIFAR-10 grows from 0.88 at width~16 to 2.26 at width~256,
whereas ensemble NLL grows only from 0.74 to 1.34---a 7$\times$ smaller
increase.  On FashionMNIST, both single-model and ensemble NLL remain low
($<0.36$) with small gains from ensembling ($<0.09$), consistent with the
easy-task regime.  Ensembles stabilize calibration across the full capacity
range.  Temperature scaling achieves lower ECE in isolation but requires
held-out data and provides no accuracy benefit.  The calibration bar chart
comparing all three methods is in Appendix~\ref{app:ece_bar}.

\subsection{Ensemble-Size Scaling Is Maintained Across Widths}
\label{sec:scaling}

Using 10 independent checkpoints per width for CIFAR-10, we bootstrap
ensemble accuracy for $M = 1, \ldots, 10$ members (500 bootstrap samples
per $M$).  Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble_scaling} shows absolute accuracy
(left) and gain over a single model (right).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../outputs/series/paper-plots/ensemble_size_scaling.png}
\caption{Ensemble accuracy (left) and gain over single model (right) as a
function of ensemble size $M$ on CIFAR-10, for each width.  Shaded bands
show $\pm 1$ std across bootstrap samples.  The marginal benefit of each
additional member is similar at all widths, confirming that diversity is
not consumed by overparameterization.}
\label{fig:ensemble_scaling}
\end{figure}

The key observation is that the shape of the gain curve is similar across
all widths.  At $M=10$, models with 38K parameters gain 7.1\,pp over a
single model, while models with 9.6M parameters gain 8.2\,pp.  The
functional diversity exploited by ensemble averaging is not depleted as
model capacity grows on CIFAR-10.

\subsection{The Diversity--Barrier Dissociation}
\label{sec:barrier}

Linear interpolation barriers measure weight-space connectivity: a zero
barrier means two minima are linearly connected without a loss spike.
Figure~\ref{fig:diversity_ci} (right panel) shows that barriers
\emph{collapse} with width on both CIFAR-10 and STL-10 (from $>1.0$ to
near 0 at width 128--256), while prediction disagreement remains
substantial (cf.\ left panel, same widths).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{../outputs/series/paper-plots/barrier_vs_diversity.png}
\caption{Weight-space connectivity (barrier) vs.\ function-space
diversity (disagreement) for each width $\times$ dataset combination.
Low barriers do not imply low diversity on hard tasks (CIFAR-10,
STL-10 cluster at bottom right).}
\label{fig:barrier_diversity}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:barrier_diversity} makes this explicit: CIFAR-10 and
STL-10 points at high width lie in the bottom-right quadrant (low barrier,
high disagreement), while FashionMNIST points stay near the top-left (high
barrier, low disagreement).  The correlation between barriers and diversity
\emph{reverses} direction across tasks.

This dissociation has a practical implication: barrier-based metrics
cannot serve as proxies for ensemble diversity.  A low barrier tells us
that the two minima are linearly connected in weight space, but it does
not tell us that the models make the same predictions.

\subsection{Matched Training Progress}
\label{sec:matched}

To separate model capacity from optimization progress, we train all widths
to a target training loss of 0.44 (the best achievable loss for width~16
in 80 epochs), using early stopping.  Wider models reach this target much
faster: width~16 requires ${\sim}56$ epochs; width~64 reaches its target
in only 10 epochs and achieves substantially lower loss (0.27), revealing
a confound.

Despite this limitation, the matched-checkpoint results are consistent:
pairwise disagreement on CIFAR-10 ranges from 21\% (width~64) to 29\%
(width~16), and ensemble gain from 2.4 to 3.7 pp.  Wider models
achieve higher accuracy with comparable or greater functional diversity.

\section{Theoretical Perspective}
\label{sec:theory}

We offer a theoretical framework explaining why task difficulty governs
functional diversity more than parameter count.

\paragraph{Epistemic vs.\ aleatoric uncertainty.}
Prediction uncertainty decomposes into \emph{aleatoric} uncertainty
(irreducible noise in the labeling process, bounded below by the Bayes
error $\varepsilon^*$) and \emph{epistemic} uncertainty (uncertainty due
to limited model capacity or training data).  On an easy task
($\varepsilon^* \approx 0$), all sufficiently good classifiers must
make nearly the same predictions: the labeling function is nearly
deterministic.  On a hard task ($\varepsilon^*$ large), high aleatoric
uncertainty means that many labeling rules are equally consistent with
the data, creating substantial room for epistemic uncertainty—and hence
for models trained from different initializations to disagree.

\begin{proposition}[Disagreement under error independence]
\label{prop:multiplicity}
Let $\varepsilon = \varepsilon^* + \delta$ be the per-model error rate of
two classifiers $f_a, f_b \in \mathcal{F}^*_\delta$ where
$\mathcal{F}^*_\delta = \{f : \mathrm{err}(f) \leq \varepsilon^* + \delta\}$.
If the two classifiers err independently on each input (conditional on
the input), then:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{disagree}(f_a, f_b)]
  \;=\; 2\varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon)
  \;\xrightarrow{\delta\to 0}\; 2\varepsilon^*(1 - \varepsilon^*).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\mathrm{disagree}(f_a, f_b) = P(f_a \mathrm{\ correct},\, f_b \mathrm{\ wrong}) + P(f_a \mathrm{\ wrong},\, f_b \mathrm{\ correct})$.
Under independence of errors conditional on $x$, $P(f_a \mathrm{\ correct},\, f_b \mathrm{\ wrong}) = (1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon$ and similarly for the other term, giving $2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)$, which is maximized at $\varepsilon = 1/2$.
\end{proof}

\noindent The key question is when error independence holds.  On an easy
task ($\varepsilon^* \approx 0$), almost all good classifiers agree, so errors
are maximally correlated and disagreement is near zero.  On a hard task with
large $\varepsilon^*$, many distinct near-optimal solutions exist (one for
each pattern of choices on confusable inputs), so different random
initializations find different solutions---errors become approximately
independent and disagreement approaches $2\varepsilon^*(1-\varepsilon^*)$.
This quantity is increasing in $\varepsilon^*$ for $\varepsilon^* < 1/2$,
formalizing why harder tasks yield higher ensemble diversity.

\paragraph{Width and the loss landscape.}
In the NTK regime, infinitely-wide networks converge to the same kernel
machine solution, which is unique for a given kernel.  Standard training,
however, operates in the feature-learning regime, where implicit biases
of SGD select among many solutions of equal training loss.  On hard tasks,
many such solutions with different error patterns co-exist, and random
initialization determines which one is found—independently of width.
Width affects how quickly and how well each solution is found, but not
the underlying functional multiplicity.

This explains the diversity--barrier dissociation: as width grows, the
loss landscape becomes flatter and minima become linearly connected (low
barriers), but because many functionally distinct near-optimal solutions
exist, independent training still lands in different ones.

\begin{remark}[Overconfidence scaling with capacity]
When a wider model finds a specific near-optimal solution via gradient
descent, it does so with sharper loss landscape geometry (flatter minima,
lower barriers), which correlates with sharper (higher-confidence)
predictive distributions.  This explains the observed ECE growth with
width on CIFAR-10: wider models commit more strongly to their particular
error pattern.  Ensemble averaging over independently chosen patterns
cancels overconfidence: the averaged distribution is more spread across
competing decisions.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Calibration and complementary errors]
Individual models are overconfident on hard tasks because they commit to
a specific error pattern while assigning high confidence to those
decisions.  When two models have complementary errors, their average
predictive distribution is spread across more of input space—reducing
overconfidence even without explicit calibration.  This explains why
ECE reduction from ensembles grows with task difficulty.
\end{remark}

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Practical implications.}
Our results support ensemble deployment for large models on hard tasks.
The claim that overparameterization makes ensembles redundant does not
hold at the scale and task range we study.  Based on our findings, we
suggest the following heuristic: if a task's best single-model accuracy
is below $\sim$85\%, ensembles provide substantial gains in both accuracy
(3--4 pp) and calibration (5--14 pp ECE reduction); if accuracy exceeds
$\sim$90\%, temperature scaling is comparably effective for calibration,
and ensembles provide diminishing gains.  The ensemble-size scaling
curves (Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble_scaling}) show that practitioners can
expect $5$--$8$ pp NLL improvement from a 10-member ensemble on CIFAR-10,
regardless of model capacity.

\paragraph{Connection to model merging.}
Our diversity--barrier dissociation is directly relevant to model merging
methods~\citep{wortsman2022model}, which average model weights directly.
On hard tasks (CIFAR-10, STL-10), low loss barriers mean that weight
interpolation between two models encounters no loss spikes, yet the models
make functionally different predictions.  This explains why model merging
tends to underperform ensembles on hard tasks: merging collapses the
functional diversity that ensembles preserve.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our primary CNN has at most $\sim$10M parameters on 32$\times$32 images;
modern large-scale models operate at $10^8$--$10^{11}$ parameters.
Whether the task-difficulty finding holds at those scales is an open and
important question.  The patch-transformer experiments were
optimization-limited and do not cleanly extend our conclusions to
attention-based architectures.  The corrupted-CIFAR-10 evaluation uses a
single severity level per corruption type; a full CIFAR-10-C evaluation
(19 corruption types, 5 severities) would strengthen the OOD claim.
Additionally, our three seeds per width may produce variance in
pairwise metrics for smaller datasets (SVHN, STL-10).  Finally, STL-10's
smaller training set (5k vs 50k for CIFAR-10) may confound capacity effects
with data efficiency effects for that dataset.

\paragraph{Ongoing and future work.}
A residual network (ResNet) CIFAR-10 sweep across the same width range is
running to test robustness of the diversity--difficulty finding across
architectures.  Other key directions include: extending to
larger-scale architectures (ViT, ResNet-50) and datasets (ImageNet,
CIFAR-100 with 100-class difficulty); studying mixed-architecture
ensembles; theoretical characterization of the diversity--barrier
dissociation; and connecting to ensemble distillation and Bayesian deep
learning.

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have shown systematically that functional diversity in deep ensembles is
governed by task difficulty rather than by model capacity.  Across six
benchmarks spanning MNIST (2.3\% pairwise disagreement) to CIFAR-10
(25--31\% disagreement), two model families, and a 250$\times$ parameter
range, pairwise prediction disagreement, ensemble accuracy gain, and
calibration benefit are consistently large on hard tasks and consistently
small on easy tasks, irrespective of width.  A secondary finding is that
overparameterization causes increasing overconfidence on hard tasks (single-model
ECE 7\% to 22\% as width grows), while ensemble averaging stabilizes
calibration across the full capacity range.  We further demonstrate
that two-member ensembles of smaller models outperform single larger models
at the same total parameter budget, and that loss-landscape weight-space
connectivity (barrier collapse) does not predict functional diversity---
barrier metrics are insufficient proxies for ensemble benefit.  These
results imply that deep ensembles remain practically valuable for large
overparameterized models whenever the task is genuinely hard.

Our code, experiment configurations, and analysis scripts are available at
\href{https://github.com/scaling-ensembles}{scaling-ensembles}.
